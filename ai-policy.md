## 1. You may use AI

You are permitted to use AI assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, or whatever
else) on the computer labs of this module.  You do not need to
ask permission, and using one carries no penalty.

Programming is changing, and these tools are now part of how it is done.  You
should learn to work with them, keeping two things in mind.

* **Marks for engagement.**  The labs carry partial marks for participation
  and engagement, not only for correct answers.  An answer that arrived correct
  and unexamined earns little.
* **The exam is closed.**  You will sit it on your own, with no assistant.  The
  hours you spend practising in the lab are what prepare you for it; handing
  them to a machine does not save you the work, it moves it to a worse moment.

This document is not a new rulebook written for a new technology; it spells out
what the five values of **academic integrity** ask of you in this situation.

## 2. The five values of Academic Integrity

### Honesty -- *present your own work truthfully*

Each lab notebook ends with an **AI use** cell.  Fill it in with one sentence
naming the tools you used and what you used them for.  For example:

> *AI use: I used Claude to explain a `TypeError` in Task 2, and to suggest the
> `zip` in Task 4, which I then rewrote.*  Or, just as good: *AI use: none.*

A submission with an honest declaration is in better standing than one
without; leaving it out when you did use an assistant would be a breach.

### Trust -- *maintain confidence of your peers*

Your lab submission is read and marked to give you feedback on **your work**,
and so that your final mark means something about you.  A notebook produced by
an assistant says something only about the assistant, and dilutes the meaning
of a CS2101 mark.

The same applies between you: do not put a classmate in the position of handing
up work that is not theirs, and do not ask them to put you there.

### Fairness -- *follow rules, ensure equal opportunities*

Every student in CS2101 may use these tools, so there is no advantage in concealing
it and no disadvantage in declaring it.  Note that explicit
rules like this are **per module**: other lecturers may take a different line.

### Respect -- *acknowledge intellectual property*

An assistant's output is built out of a great deal of code written by other
people, and arrives with none of the attribution attached.  You cannot fix that,
but you can be straight about where your own work came from: where a substantial
part of an answer came from somewhere else (an assistant, a web search, a
classmate, a worked example in the notes) say so, in the same sentence as the
declaration -- the standard for academic work in every other module.

### Responsibility -- *take ownership of your learning*

Every lab submission implicitly claims that the work in it is **your own
work**.  That claim does not depend on which tools you used, but on three
things:

* **You understand it.**  You can explain what every line of your code does, and
  why it is there.
* **You decided it.**  The choices in the notebook (how to break the problem
  up, which construction to use, what to call things) are choices you made and
  can defend.  Accepting a suggestion is a decision only if you understood the
  alternatives.
* **You could do it again.**  Given a similar problem and no assistant, you
  would know how to solve it, perhaps more slowly.

If all three hold, the work is yours, however much help you had.  If any one of
them fails, it is not.

**You may be asked.**  If we cannot tell from a submission whether you
understand it, you may be asked to talk us through it.

## 3. Some advice on using AI well

* **Ask it to explain rather than to write.**  *"Why does my loop stop one step
  early?"* teaches you something.  *"Write me a function that ..."* does not.
* **Write your own attempt first**, then ask for a critique of it.  You will
  learn far more from a comment on your code than from a replacement for it.
* **Ask for a second way of doing it**, and work out which you prefer and why.
  That is a real programming skill.
* **Run everything.**  These tools are confidently wrong on a regular basis.
  Code that looks plausible and returns a number is not thereby correct.
* **Watch for answers from the wrong course.**  An assistant does not know where
  we are.  It will happily hand you `pandas` in Lab 2, a library we meet in
  Notebook 7, or a one-line `numpy` trick for an exercise whose whole purpose is
  the `for` loop.  A solution using something we have not covered means you have
  not done the exercise that was set.
* **Use it to test yourself.**  Ask it to quiz you on a notebook, or to set you
  three variations on a task you have just finished.  This is the use that helps
  most before the exam, and the one students think of last.
